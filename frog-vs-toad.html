Frog vs. toad: how to tell them apart

Toads are technically a type of frog, but in everyday use "frog" and "toad" describe two noticeably different builds. If you've spotted something and aren't sure which you're looking at, four things settle it almost every time: skin, body shape, legs, and where you found it.

The four tells

Feature Frog Toad
Skin Smooth, moist, often shiny Dry, bumpy, almost leathery
Body shape Slender, built for sitting at water's edge Rounder, squatter, built for time on dry land
Legs Long, powerful — built for leaping Shorter, less developed — mostly hops and walks
Typical habitat In or very near water most of the time Spends most of the year on land, returns to water mainly to breed

A closer look at each tell

Skin

This is usually the fastest way to tell them apart on sight. Frog skin stays moist and smooth because frogs lose water easily and need to stay near it. Toad skin is dry and covered in small bumps, which helps toads survive longer stretches away from water. The bumps are glands, not warts, and handling a toad will not give you warts — that's an old myth, not a real risk.

Body shape and legs

A frog's long back legs are built for a big, explosive leap away from danger, which is why frogs are usually seen jumping rather than walking. A toad's shorter legs are better suited to a slower hop or an actual walk, and toads rely more on blending into dry ground than on leaping away. If you see something moving in short hops or even walking a few steps at a time, that alone is a strong sign it's a toad.

Where you found it

Location is often the clearest clue of all. If it was sitting at the water's edge or swimming, you were almost certainly looking at a frog. If you found it well away from water — in a garden, under a porch light, or crossing a sidewalk — a toad is far more likely, since toads only need standing water briefly each year to breed.

Specific to Iowa

In Iowa, the species you're most likely to run into on land is the American Toad, dry-skinned and bumpy, often found around house foundations and gardens at night hunting insects. Near water, the common sightings are green frogs, leopard frogs, and bullfrogs — all smooth-skinned, long-legged, and rarely far from a pond's edge.
