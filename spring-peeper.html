Species profile

Spring peeper frog

If the sound you're hearing is a short, high-pitched "peep" repeated roughly once a second — especially in March or April, before most other frogs have started — you're hearing a spring peeper. It's often the very first frog call of the year in Iowa, and for many people it's the sound that means winter is actually over.

The call

A single spring peeper gives a clear, high whistle, repeated at a steady pace, somewhere close to the pitch of a bird chirp but thinner and more piercing. On its own it can sound almost like a smoke detector's low-battery chirp. In a chorus — and peepers almost always call in large groups — hundreds of overlapping peeps blend into a ringing, sleigh-bell-like wall of sound that can be heard from well over a quarter mile away on a still night.

What it looks like

Spring peepers are small, rarely longer than an inch and a half, and easy to miss even when a chorus is deafening — they're usually hidden in grass or low brush near water rather than out in the open. Color ranges from tan to gray to light brown, with a dark X-shaped marking across the back that gives the species its scientific name, Pseudacris crucifer (crucifer meaning "cross-bearer"). The X is the most reliable visual field mark if you do manage to spot one.

When and where to hear one

Peepers are typically the earliest callers of the year, starting as soon as nighttime temperatures climb above the high 30s to low 40s, often while there's still ice at the pond's edge. Calling usually peaks in late March through April and tapers off by late May as other species take over. Listen near shallow, temporary pools, flooded ditches, and the edges of wooded wetlands — peepers favor shallow water with plenty of low vegetation to call from.

Easy to confuse with

The boreal chorus frog gives a call in a similar pitch range but sounds more like a fingernail dragged along the teeth of a comb — a rising, rough trill rather than a clean, repeated peep. If the sound is a single sharp note repeated on a steady beat, it's a peeper; if it's a short rising buzz, it's more likely a chorus frog. Our night trilling guide covers the longer, buzzier callers if that sounds closer to what you're hearing.

Quick recap

  1. High, short, repeated peep, close to once per second.
  2. First species calling in late winter and early spring, often before the ice is fully gone.
  3. Tiny — under two inches — with a dark X on its back if you can get a look at one.
  4. Calls in large, loud choruses from shallow, temporary water.
